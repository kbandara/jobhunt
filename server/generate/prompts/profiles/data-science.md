# Profile: data-science — data science and machine learning roles

The reader is technical, and usually the person you would report to or work
alongside. They are not reading for polish. They are reading for whether you have
built things that worked, whether you know why they worked, and whether you would
notice when one stopped working.

Section order: Summary, Technical Skills, Experience, Projects, Education,
Selected Publications. Around two pages — a guide, not a limit; nothing the
advertisement asked for is dropped to hit it and nothing is padded to reach it.

## Why skills come second here

Unusually high, and deliberately. A data science reader scans for the stack before
they read anything else, and burying it under two pages of prose means it is
checked last or not at all. Keep it factual: the languages, libraries and
infrastructure that appear in the records, grouped so the advertisement's own
must-haves are findable at a glance.

Do not list a tool that appears in no record. A skills section is the easiest
place in a CV to write something nobody can support, and the easiest place for an
interviewer to start.

## What a bullet should say

Three things, in this order: **what you built, what it did, and how you know.**

- "Built a churn model" says nothing. "Built a churn model that the retention
  team ran monthly; it moved their targeting from tenure buckets to predicted
  risk" says what changed.
- Method belongs in the bullet only when it was the interesting part. Naming a
  gradient-boosted tree is worth a clause; naming the library is worth nothing
  unless the advertisement asked for it.
- Where a record has a number, use it and cite the record it came from. Where it
  does not, describe the change without inventing a figure — a bullet that says
  what moved is stronger than one with a percentage nobody can check.

## Projects are evidence, not decoration

This profile gives projects their own section because in this field they often
carry more weight than a job title. A tool other people use, a pipeline that
still runs, an analysis somebody acted on — these answer "can you finish
something" better than a role description does.

Treat an unpaid or side project exactly like paid work: what it does, who used
it, what it required. If nobody used it, say what it demonstrates rather than
implying adoption.

## Research and publications

Academic work appears, framed as what it produced rather than as a contribution
to a literature. A paper is worth a line when it supports a claim the
advertisement cares about — a method, a dataset, a result. At most three, one
line each.

A PhD in progress is a strength here and should read as one: it is three years of
independent technical work with a deadline. Say what it involved. Never state or
imply that it is finished when the profile says it is not.

## Vocabulary

Use the advertisement's word for a thing whenever a record supports the work. If
the ad says "experimentation" and the record says "A/B tests", say
experimentation. If the ad names a method the records do not contain, say nothing
about it — its absence is reported as a gap, which is more useful than a sentence
that dances around it.

Specialist terms are fine here in a way they are not in the `industry` profile:
the reader is technical. What is still not fine is a term used to sound competent
rather than to describe something that happened.
